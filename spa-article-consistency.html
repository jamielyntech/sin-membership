The Body Doesn't Ask to Be Fixed Once

Higher Purpose Wellness Spa Wellness Journal 7 min read

There is a question I hear often, usually near the end of a session, when a client is still a little warm and quiet on the table. It sounds like a compliment but it carries something else underneath it. The question is: "What has changed in all the years you have been doing this?"

The client usually means the techniques. The equipment. The modalities. And yes, those have evolved. But what actually changed was something harder to name. It was my understanding of what a body needs, not just in a single hour, but across months and years. That understanding did not come from any course or certification. It came from showing up, session after session, and paying close attention.

Thirty-seven years of that attention taught me one thing above everything else. Consistency reveals what intensity alone never can.

What a single intense session can and cannot do

A skilled session can do remarkable things. It can soften a shoulder that has been locked tight for weeks. It can coax a nervous system out of a high-alert state and into something quieter. A client who walks in guarded and contracted can leave feeling open, lighter, more at ease in their own skin. That matters. That is real.

But a single session, however thorough, is still just one conversation. The body has been having a much longer conversation with itself, shaped by sleep patterns and stress and old habits and the particular way someone holds their breath when the phone rings. One visit can interrupt that pattern briefly. It cannot rewrite it.

I learned this not from a textbook but from watching the same bodies return over years. The clients who came in twice and then disappeared would come back six months later carrying the same tension in the same exact place, as if the work had never happened. Not because the session failed. Because the body had simply gone back to the only rhythm it knew.

What repetition teaches a body

The body learns through repetition. This is not a philosophy. It is how tissue and nervous system actually function.

Research increasingly suggests that repeated massage does more than create temporary relaxation. A 2026 review by researchers James Chmiel and Donata Kurpas, synthesizing 47 brain-imaging studies using EEG, fMRI, and fNIRS, found evidence that consistent manual therapy may contribute to longer-term changes in how the brain processes stress, pain, and internal bodily signals. Rather than simply responding to each session as a separate event, the nervous system appears to become more familiar with the experience of safety and regulation. Over time, calm becomes less of a surprise and more of a place the body remembers how to return to.

When a body experiences calm, supported stillness again and again, it begins to recognize that state as accessible. Not just as something that happened once on a table somewhere. As a place it knows how to find.

Clients who have maintained a consistent rhythm of care over years carry something different into their sessions. There is a quality of ease in the first few minutes that newer clients take much longer to reach. The body remembers. It remembers the warmth, the weight, the breath slowing down. It arrives already leaning toward that state rather than having to be talked into it.

That accumulated memory is not a small thing. It is, in many ways, the whole point.

The thing intensity misses

Wellness culture tends to reward intensity. The deep tissue work that leaves you sore for three days. The cleanse that asks everything of you for a week. The retreat that promises transformation in a long weekend. These experiences can be powerful. I am not dismissing them.

But they operate on a different logic than long-term care. Intensity asks the body to respond to something big. Consistency asks the body to trust something steady. And trust, in a nervous system context, is not built through peak experiences. It is built through repetition. Through the quiet accumulation of sessions where nothing dramatic happened but something gently shifted. Where the neck was a little less guarded than last time. Where the breath came a little easier.

Stephen Porges, whose research on the autonomic nervous system has shaped how somatic practitioners think about safety, described neuroception as the nervous system's continuous, subconscious scan for threat or safety in the body and environment. The system is always asking: is this safe? The answer does not arrive from a single compelling data point. It arrives from a pattern. The same space. The same practitioner. The same quality of attention. Over time, the nervous system stops bracing against the unknown and begins anticipating safety instead. That shift does not happen in a single session, however well executed.

The body doesn't ask to be fixed once. It asks to be listened to consistently.

That is the line I keep coming back to after thirty-seven years. Not because it sounds good but because I have seen it play out in real time, in real bodies, over and over again.

What changes when you stop chasing the big fix

One of the quietest shifts in a long client relationship is when we both stop waiting for the breakthrough session. When the goal stops being "get this sorted" and becomes something more like "stay in a good working relationship with this body."

That shift changes what we pay attention to. Instead of asking what is wrong today, the more useful question becomes: what has been building? What is this body moving toward, and what might be getting in the way? A practitioner who has seen the same person across seasons, across years, across different chapters of their life, can hear those answers in ways that a first visit simply cannot offer.

This is not about the practitioner having some special gift. It is about the information that only time provides. The pattern you notice in a third session. The change you feel in a tenth. The thing a client mentions almost in passing that you recognize because you have been paying attention long enough to know it is not nothing.

Showing up is the practice

I have worked with clients who came in feeling exhausted and came out feeling restored. I have also worked with clients who came in feeling fine and left feeling something they could not quite name, a quietness, a sense of being a little more at home in themselves. Neither of those sessions was more valuable than the other. Both were part of a longer arc.

Wellness, from where I stand after thirty-seven years, is not a condition you arrive at. It is a relationship you maintain. Some weeks that relationship requires more attention. Some weeks it is just a check-in, a reminder that you have a body and that body deserves to be heard. Either way, the showing up is the practice. The rhythm is the medicine.
